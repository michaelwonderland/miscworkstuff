Introduction
Why this exists

LinkedIn will spend your whole budget. That isn’t the same as reaching people.

Most LinkedIn advice treats it like Meta with job titles. It isn’t, and the difference is expensive.

LinkedIn prices delivery its own way. Small audiences, fragmented accounts and tired creative all push your CPM up, while your budget keeps getting spent as if nothing happened. The platform won’t flag it. Campaign Manager will show a campaign pacing on schedule with a steady CTR. You find the problem in the delivery data, or you don’t find it at all.

These are the lessons that held up after €1M of spend, in four pillars.

Pillar 01 · Audience size drives your costs
01

A (“perfect”) audience is too expensive to reach

Every targeting layer shrinks the pool and raises the price of every impression inside it.

Account list and job title and seniority and company size feels precise. To LinkedIn’s auction, it’s a small pool that every advertiser in your category is also bidding on. A “top of funnel” campaign built on an account list isn’t prospecting. It’s retargeting, and it gets priced like retargeting.

The answer isn’t Meta-style broad targeting either. Audience Expansion and the Audience Network both drift away from your buyer. The right audience is wide enough to deliver and tight enough to stay relevant: job function plus company size, then stop.

Signs you’re doing this

  • A “prospecting” audience small enough to list in a spreadsheet
  • Account lists combined with AND logic on titles and seniority

Do this instead

  • Define the audience by job function and company size
  • Use account lists to measure engagement, not to drive conversions
Rule of thumbIf you can name most of the companies in your prospecting audience, it’s a retargeting audience.

02

Your audience size sets your budget, not the other way round

Past a certain spend, LinkedIn doesn’t find new people. It charges you more for the ones you already reach.

Unless you’ve set a bid cap, LinkedIn will spend your full daily budget whether or not there’s anyone new to show the ad to. When the pool runs dry, it serves fewer impressions at a higher price to hit the number you gave it.

In Campaign Manager this looks healthy: budget spent, CTR steady. The only sign is in delivery, with impressions falling and CPM rising while spend stays flat.

Signs you’re doing this

  • Spend flat, impressions down, CPM up
  • CTR holding steady, so the ad isn’t the problem. The pool is used up.

Do this instead

  • Budget for 3–4 impressions per person per week, then stop adding money
  • To spend more, widen the audience rather than raising the budget
Rule of thumbUnderpacing on a small audience isn’t a problem to fix. It’s the platform telling you the truth.

03

Audience size decides how long your ads last

Creative doesn’t wear out on a schedule. It wears out at the rate your audience sees it.

A small list sees the same ad again and again, so it tires in days. A broad audience can keep the same ad working for weeks. Same creative, different shelf life.

So the narrower you target, the bigger the creative pipeline you need, and you have to plan it before launch. By the time CTR tells you an ad is tired, CPM has usually been telling you for a while.

Signs you’re doing this

  • Weekly frequency above ~3 on the same ad
  • Your launch-week winner becoming your worst ad

Do this instead

  • Small lists: launch 8–10 variants, rotate every 5–7 days
  • Broad audiences: review creative from week 4
Rule of thumbThe smaller the audience, the more creative it eats.

04

A CPM spike means your ad has run out of people

Check CPM daily, ad by ad. A jump to 4x its own baseline means the audience is exhausted.

When an ad runs out of new people, LinkedIn serves it less often. If you’re not bid-capped, it still spends your full budget, and the only way to do both is to charge more per impression. That’s the spike. It rarely corrects itself, so pause or refresh the ad the same day.

Pillar 01 · Audience size drives your costs
05

More ad sets on one audience raise the price for all of them

Ad sets that target the same people enter the same auction. You end up bidding against yourself.

It happens gradually. Every new offer, market version or test gets its own small ad set, until a dozen of them sit on minimum budgets, all aimed at the same retargeting pool. Each one pushes up the price for the others.

The giveaway is CPM climbing while CTR holds. There’s nothing wrong with the ads. The account is competing with itself.

Signs you’re doing this

  • Many ad sets sitting on LinkedIn’s minimum daily budget
  • Near-identical versions of one ad split across campaigns

Do this instead

  • Fewer ad sets, properly funded, with 5–6 ads each
  • Split a campaign out only when its goal is genuinely different
Rule of thumbOne audience, one objective, one campaign.

Pillar 02 · What LinkedIn is for
06

LinkedIn is a reach channel, not a demo machine

Most of the conversions LinkedIn reports come from people who never clicked.

A view-through conversion means someone saw an ad, didn’t click, and converted later through another route. They make up the bulk of what LinkedIn reports, and with long attribution windows it will credit campaigns that were already paused.

Judge LinkedIn on cost per demo and it will always look expensive. Judge it on its own conversion column and it will look better than it is. Neither shows what it’s good at: putting you in front of the right people, repeatedly, before they’re in market.

Signs you’re doing this

  • Paused campaigns still “converting”
  • A long list of tracked conversion events, from video views to downloads

Do this instead

  • Report post-click and view-through conversions separately
  • Track 2–3 conversion events, and measure reach within your ideal buyers
Rule of thumbIf nobody clicked, treat the conversion as a hypothesis, not a result.

Pillar 02 · What LinkedIn is for
07

You don’t need bottom-of-funnel ads on LinkedIn

Expecting to use LinkedIn like Meta is the most expensive mistake in this guide.

Meta can take broad targeting and a conversion goal and find the buyers. LinkedIn can’t, not at the audience sizes B2B works with. Pushing demo-request ads to a narrow audience combines every problem in pillar one.

What works is a steady mix of two kinds of content, shown to an audience of your ideal buyers that’s wide enough to sustain it. Sales closes the demo. LinkedIn makes sure the buyer already knows your name.

Always on

Evergreen

Benchmarks, guides, tools and templates people keep coming back to. It runs all year and gives people a reason to remember you.

In bursts

Seasonal

Timely reasons to care: regulation changes, budget and planning cycles, industry moments. Short bursts that make you relevant right now.

Rule of thumbOffer something worth having. Nobody books a demo from the feed.

Pillar 03 · What earns attention in the feed
08

People beat logos

LinkedIn is a feed of people. Ads from people fit in. Ads from a company page look like ads.

Thought Leader ads, which are sponsored posts from real people at your company, get more engagement and cheaper delivery than the same message from a brand page. They also last longer: on a broad top-of-funnel audience, a strong post can keep working for months.

Longer isn’t forever. They wear out like any other creative; it just takes more time. Watch their CPM the same way (lesson 04) and refresh when it spikes.

Signs you’re doing this

  • The whole budget behind company-page posts
  • Thought Leader posts left running for months with nobody checking CPM

Do this instead

  • Build a small group of people whose posts you can sponsor
  • Promote posts that are already performing organically
Rule of thumbPut spend behind people, not the page. Then refresh them like everything else.

Pillar 03 · What earns attention in the feed
09

Document ads get 6x the clicks

Give people the value in the feed, and LinkedIn and your audience both reward you for it.

6x

The click-through rate of the same message as a single image ad, at around a fifth of the cost per click.

A document ad is a swipeable PDF that people read without leaving LinkedIn. Every swipe counts as engagement, it reads better than a landing page on a phone, and it keeps people on the platform, which is exactly what LinkedIn wants.

If your offer is a guide, a report or a checklist, show a real preview in the feed instead of hiding everything behind a click. Give away the first few pages and ask for the rest.

Rule of thumbGive value in the feed first. Ask for the click after.

Pillar 04 · Work with the algorithm
10

Every big change sends you back to the learning phase

Change everything at once and LinkedIn starts learning from scratch.

Replace all your proven ads in one go, or move a large share of budget overnight, and delivery drops while costs jump. Recovery can take one to two weeks. Most teams read the dip as “the new creative doesn’t work”, change something else, and reset it again.

Signs you’re doing this

  • A performance drop straight after a “refresh”
  • Several changes made in the same week

Do this instead

  • Add new ads alongside the winners; retire the old ones gradually
  • Move budget in steps of about 25%, a week apart
Rule of thumbChange one thing, then give it a week.

Pillar 04 · Work with the algorithm
11

Don’t bid for conversions on campaigns shorter than the learning phase

A five-day burst for an event, a launch or an account-based push ends before the algorithm has learnt who converts. You pay the price of conversion bidding and never get the benefit. For bursts, bid for reach or engagement and let the timing and the message do the work.

Rule of thumbShort burst? Buy reach, not conversions.
12

LinkedIn’s algorithm needs more signal than a small audience can give

This is the other half of lesson 07. Meta can take a broad audience and find the buyers inside it. LinkedIn’s algorithm isn’t that sophisticated. Give it a small audience, or a small market, plus a conversion goal, and it doesn’t find anyone new. It bids up the price of the few people it has, and most of the “conversions” it records are view-throughs.

Where the audience is small, optimise for reach or website visits and measure conversions separately.

Rule of thumbSmall pool plus conversion bidding means paying a premium for view-throughs.

# Facebook Ads Testing: The Big-Swing Handbook

Facebook ads testing in 2026 means fewer tests with more in each one. Put 10 to 25 visually different ads in a test ad set, let Meta decide where the money goes for about two weeks, and judge the result on purchases and cost per purchase. Winners move to a separate scaling campaign, and everything else switches off with a written reason.

That is close to the opposite of the textbook, which says to change one variable, hold the rest equal and split spend evenly. That method assumes a delivery system Meta has since replaced.

The cost of following it is quiet. A test of four near-identical ads can spend $2,000 over two weeks and teach you nothing.

As far as Meta could tell, it was one ad.

This handbook covers testing at every budget tier, how many ads to put in each ad set, the math on spend per ad and days to signal, and how to decide what to iterate and what to kill. It leans on creative strategist Dara Denney's current testing protocol, and on the part we know best from building Draper Ads inside a paid social agency: getting 20 ads live without losing the afternoon.

## How should you test Facebook ads now?

**Test wide, not narrow: one testing campaign, 10 to 25 genuinely different ads per test ad set, a two-week window, and decisions made only on primary metrics.**

Three terms before we go further:

- **Big-swing test:** a test ad set of 10 to 25 ads that look clearly different from one another, where Meta allocates spend and you read the outcome after the window closes.
- **Test window:** the fixed period, about two weeks, before you're allowed to call a winner or a loser.
- **Primary metrics:** spend, purchases, cost per purchase and ROAS (return on ad spend). The only numbers allowed to trigger a scale or a kill.

The whole system runs on two campaigns. A testing campaign holds the big-swing tests, one ad set per test, and a scaling campaign holds the proven winners.

Keep them apart. In her [video on testing at every budget](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7knQyPYLmfo), Denney says she has repeatedly seen a creative test dropped into a performing campaign drag that campaign down.

Here's what that looks like for a skincare brand spending $1,000 a day. One test ad set goes live each week with 20 statics or 12 UGC videos.

Two weeks later, the two or three ads that took spend and hit target cost per purchase get duplicated into the scaling campaign. The rest are switched off, each with a one-line note on why it lost.

That's the shape. The rest of this page is the reasoning, the sizing and the math.

## Why single-variable tests stopped working

**Meta's retrieval system, Andromeda, only treats visual difference as creative diversity, so a test where the ads look the same is a test Meta can't see.**

The orthodox advice is familiar. Change one word in the hook, keep the visual identical, and you'll know exactly what caused the lift.

It's clean. It's how you'd design a lab experiment.

Denney was asked this directly in [a June 2026 video on hooks](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=I8tXqqfjIX4&t=551s). Should you change only one word so the variable is clean? Her answer was "Absolutely not. I am actually taking much bigger swings in my variation testing."

The reason is mechanical, not stylistic. Andromeda is the step that narrows millions of ads down to the pool a given person might see, and our [explainer on the Andromeda update](/blog/meta-andromeda-update) covers the full pipeline.

Denney [learned on a visit to Meta](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=kxLjzws-pNg&t=243s) that Andromeda registers unique visual difference as diversity, and that messaging-only changes don't count, even when they'd appeal to different people. She notes it applies mainly to Advantage+ campaigns.

Seed Health's creative strategist puts the same idea as a two-second rule. If you can't tell two ads apart at a glance, the algorithm may not either.

Meaning, a single-variable test isn't a cleaner version of a big-swing test. To Meta it's one ad with a few spare copies.

Compare two test ad sets with the same budget:

- **Narrow test:** six versions of one founder video, each with a different first line of on-screen text. Same face, same kitchen, same lighting.
- **Big-swing test:** the founder video, a customer testimonial filmed in a car, a green-screen reaction to a competitor, a us-vs-them static, a press-quote static and an illustrated before-and-after.

The first asks Meta to choose between six identical-looking ads. The second hands it six different doors to six different buyers.

Only the second gives the system something to learn from.

Run messaging tests anyway, because words still move performance. Wrap each new message in a new visual so the test registers at all.

There's one place the textbook still holds. At $1M+ a month, Denney says [variation tests get more granular](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7knQyPYLmfo&t=853s), with smaller differences, because the account already knows what broadly works. Fine-tuning a proven winner is a different job from finding one.

## How many ads per ad set?

**Put 10 to 25 ads in each test ad set: 20 to 25 for statics, 10 to 15 for video and UGC, and never fewer than 10.**

That's Denney's current sizing, [set out in August 2025](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MmXb2JmWDj8&t=121s) and up from the 2 to 4 variations per test she recommended in 2024. If an account still runs three or four ads per test ad set, it's on the old playbook.

| Test type | Ads per test ad set | The rule inside the ad set |
|---|---|---|
| Statics | 20 to 25 | No more than 2 or 3 variations of any one concept. The other 20 or so should look nothing alike. |
| Video and UGC | 10 to 15 | 3 to 6 hook variants per video, each a big swing |
| Any test | 10 minimum | Every ad needs a written reason it could win |

The "2 or 3 variations" cap matters as much as the headline number.

Twenty-five ads made from three concepts is a narrow test in disguise.

Why stop at 25? Every ad you add splits the same budget into thinner slices, and past a point most of them never get shown enough to mean anything. The math section below shows where that point sits for your budget.

A quick litmus test before launch is to **print the thumbnails on one page and squint.** If you can't count at least 10 distinct-looking ads, cut the copies and brief more concepts.

## Testing at every budget tier

**Structure follows spend.** Denney is blunt that there's no single right way to test on Meta, and [splits her own approach into three tiers](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7knQyPYLmfo).

The ad-set sizing above applies to all three. What changes is the campaign setup, the budget per test and how fine the variations get.

| Tier | Monthly spend | Campaigns | Test budget | Variation style |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Lean | $5K to $30K | One sales campaign, Advantage+ on | Shared across the campaign | Big swings, mostly statics |
| Growth | ~$30K to $50K+ | Testing + scaling | $100 to $200 per test ad set per day | Big swings |
| Scale | $1M+ | Testing + scaling (still one testing campaign) | ~$200 per test per day, 7 days or $1,000 | Finer variations on proven concepts |

### 1. Lean: one campaign, new ad set every week

At $5K to $30K a month, run a single sales campaign that optimizes for purchases, with campaign budget and Advantage+ on.

Denney says she ["completely changed" her mind](https://www.youtube.com/shorts/PVoC7H44A5w) here. Instead of 4 to 6 near-variations, each test ad set now holds 10 to 15+ different creatives, and a new one launches every week, often themed by format, such as an all-image week.

Start with statics. They're cheaper and faster to make, and they tell you which messages convert before you pay for video.

Don't switch ads off unless they miss your cost-per-purchase goal and are eating a disproportionate share of spend.

**Takeaway:** at this tier, the campaign is the test, so don't split a small budget across structures.

### 2. Growth: a separate testing campaign

Around $1,000 a day, add a manual creative testing campaign next to the scaling one. Each test gets its own ad set at $100 to $200 a day.

Winners get duplicated into the scaling campaign, which turns into a campaign of winners.

Whether the testing campaign uses campaign or ad set budgets is "a media buyer decision," in Denney's words. She uses ad set budgets herself for a faster read, and we cover the trade-off in [campaign budget vs ad set budget](/blog/campaign-budget-vs-ad-set-budget).

**Takeaway:** this is the tier where launching volume jumps, which we'll come back to.

### 3. Scale: same structure, more tests

Accounts spending $1M+ a month still tend to run one testing campaign, Denney says, even at $5M a month. Tests typically launch at $200 a day and run about seven days or until $1,000 is spent.

Cost caps are common, because the target cost per purchase is already known.

The difference is volume, at 10 to 20 or more new tests a week. And as covered above, the variations get finer.

**Takeaway:** the testing structure barely changes from growth to scale. The team behind it does.

## The math of a test: spend per ad and days to signal

**A test is finished when it has bought enough purchases to read, not when it hits a date.** So work the date out from your budget before you launch.

Here's the rule of thumb we use, and it's our planning heuristic, not a Meta rule. Aim for roughly 50 purchases at the ad set level before you call the test.

That number is also where Meta's own learning phase is designed to settle.

> **Days to signal = (50 × target cost per purchase) ÷ daily test budget**

Take a growth-tier account, with these labeled inputs:

- Target cost per purchase: $40
- Daily budget for the test ad set: $150
- Ads in the test: 20 statics

The test needs about 50 × $40 = $2,000 of spend. At $150 a day, that's 13.3 days.

Call it two weeks, which is where Denney's window lands too.

Now the spend per ad. Spread evenly, $2,000 across 20 ads is $100 each, or 2.5 purchases' worth.

That already looks thin. But Meta won't spread it evenly, and it isn't trying to.

In [one account Denney walked through](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=CCsty8R0UaA&t=856s), a single ad took about 75% of the campaign's spend. Say your test is less lopsided and three ads take 70% of the $2,000. The other 17 share $600, about $35 each.

That's less than one purchase per ad.

So most ads in a big-swing test never get a full read, and that's by design. In [Denney's breakdown of Seed Health's testing](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hGCdGNP5oGE), the team had been forcing even spend across every ad to get full data on each one; they dropped it because it pushed money into creative the algorithm had already judged less scalable.

Meta's early choice of where to spend is part of the result.

Now flex one variable. Keep everything the same but sell a product with an $80 target cost per purchase:

- Spend needed: 50 × $80 = $4,000
- Days at $150 a day: 26.7

Suddenly your two-week test is a four-week test.

You have three honest options. Raise the test budget to about $285 a day, cut the ads to 12 to 15 so fewer share it, or accept a directional read at day 14 and say so in your notes.

And the lean tier? At $5,000 a month, about $165 a day across the whole account, with a $40 target, you buy roughly four purchases a day. That's around 29 a week, short of 50.

This is why the lean tier runs one campaign. Split $165 a day across a testing and a scaling campaign and neither has enough to learn from.

**Litmus test:** if your days to signal comes out above 21, change the budget or the ad count before launch, not after.

## How to read the results

**Read in two passes: what is working, then why.** [Denney's framework](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=CCsty8R0UaA&t=670s) is that every analysis answers exactly those two questions, and that the core mistake is trying to analyze and optimize at the same time.

Finish reading before you touch anything.

**What is working** uses primary metrics only. Sort the test ad set by spend, highest first, then check purchases, cost per purchase and ROAS on the ads Meta chose to fund.

A winner is an ad that took meaningful spend and hit your cost-per-purchase target.

An ad that got $12 isn't a loser. It's an ad Meta passed on.

**Why it's working** uses storytelling metrics like hook rate, hold rate, unique outbound click-through rate, frequency and CPM. These explain a result, but they never trigger an action on their own.

That split is the guardrail. Don't pause an ad for a low CTR, and don't scale one for a high hook rate.

Under broad delivery, a lower CTR can mean nothing more than a wider audience. Our post on [what a good hook rate is](/blog/what-is-a-good-hook-rate) covers how to use hook rate as a diagnostic instead.

Here's the kind of readout to avoid, close to one Denney uses as her own bad example. "Version B won with 1.2 ROAS, 1.64% CTR and a 21% hook rate, so we'll make more variants of B."

It's a table, not an analysis. A real one adds three things the numbers can't say on their own:

- **Qualitative:** the format, the creator, the setting and the message of the winner.
- **Breakdowns:** age, gender, placement. A winner skewing 50+ is a brief for more 50+ creative, not a mistake to fix.
- **Comparison:** against your own past winners and against competitor creative.

Then write it up as one paragraph covering what won, why we think it won and what we'll make next.

## When to iterate and when to kill

**Iterate on winners until they stop working. Almost never iterate on losers.**

Denney's 2026 correction is aimed at the volume era she helped start. The problem [she sees most now](https://x.com/DenneyDara/status/2041506258008613177) is accounts chasing diversity and volume while "failing to double down on what is working."

She presents [Caraway's split](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Q_hhAfjEcW0&t=489s) approvingly, at about 70% iterations and 30% net-new concepts, with "iteration" defined broadly.

Iteration means a proven concept in a new visual container. Four examples:

- Re-brief a different creator on the winning script.
- Cut new angles from footage that already tested well.
- Turn a winning video message into a static, or the reverse.
- Run the winning hook across a different format.

Every one of those connects back to Andromeda. An iteration has to look different enough to count as a new ad, or you're back to the narrow test.

Use these rules at the end of each test window:

1. **Scale** ads that took spend and beat your target. Duplicate them into the scaling campaign. Denney raises budgets about 20% every two to three days on what's working.
2. **Iterate** on each winner in the next test, and keep going until the concept stops producing winners.
3. **Kill** ads that missed the target after the window closes, and write down why. Caraway's team documents a reason for every loss.
4. **Give a loser one more go** only when you can name a specific execution mistake, such as a hook that buried the product. Stop after two or three tries at a losing concept.
5. **Leave unfunded ads alone.** If Meta barely spent on them, you have no read. Don't iterate on them either.

When a winner fades after a strong run, check it for fatigue before you kill it.

Our guide to [what ad fatigue is](/blog/what-is-ad-fatigue) covers the signs and the fix, which is usually fresh iterations of the same concept.

## The part nobody budgets for: launching the test

**A 20-ad test is a launching problem before it's a creative problem.**

Ads Manager builds campaign first, then ad set, then ad. Building one ad from scratch inside one ad set takes 17+ clicks.

That count excludes typing the ad name and the UTM parameters.

Run the growth-tier plan from this page, assuming every ad is built from scratch:

- 20 statics in one test ad set: 20 × 17 = 340 clicks
- One new test a week: 340 × 4 = 1,360 clicks a month
- Plus 20 names and 20 sets of UTMs per test, typed by hand

Now the scale tier. Say 15 tests a week at 12 ads each, which sits inside Denney's 10 to 20+ range.

That's 180 ads × 17 = 3,060 clicks a week, before you move a single winner into the scaling campaign.

You know where those hours come from. They come out of briefs, analysis and the next concept.

## Recap

- **Big-swing test:** 10 to 25 visually different ads per test ad set. If you can't tell two ads apart in two seconds, Meta probably can't either.
- **How many ads per ad set:** 20 to 25 statics, 10 to 15 videos, 10 minimum, and no more than 2 or 3 variations of any one concept.
- **Budget tiers:** under $30K a month, one campaign and a new ad set weekly. From $1,000 a day, a separate testing campaign at $100 to $200 per test. At $1M+, the same shape with more tests and finer variations.
- **Days to signal:** 50 × target cost per purchase ÷ daily test budget. Above 21 days, change the budget or the ad count before launch.
- **Reading:** primary metrics decide, storytelling metrics explain, and an unfunded ad is not a loser.
- **Iterate vs kill:** iterate winners until they stop working, kill losers with a written reason, cap a losing concept at two or three tries.

The test that teaches you most is the widest one you can fund and launch every week.

# What Are UGC Ads?

**UGC ads are paid ads built from content that looks like a real person made it: usually a creator or customer filmed on a phone, talking to camera about a product they use.** UGC stands for user-generated content. On Meta, a typical one is a 30-second Reel where someone opens with a problem, shows the product fixing it, and tells you where to buy.

That's the definition.

The more useful question is why UGC ads work, because the answer most teams were given is the one the best practitioners have dropped.

Below: what makes UGC ads convert, the formula behind them, nine example structures, how to brief and find creators, why they wear out, and where AI avatars fit. We lean throughout on creative strategist Dara Denney, who has spent years analyzing which creator content moves results on Meta.

## Why do UGC ads work?

The standard explanation is that people trust people who look like them.

Dara used to hear it all the time. Then, in her [UGC formula video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=yNTlVKvuX0o&t=62s), she said the jig is up: viewers know most UGC creators are paid to endorse. What still converts is a creator they like and believe.

We call this **borrowed trust: a UGC ad works when the viewer trusts the person on screen, and the format only gives that trust somewhere to land.**

That changes where you spend effort.

Dara puts it bluntly in her [2026 creator system video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_VtpfVeB2Kc): nine times out of 10, performance comes down to the creator. Not the hook, the brief or the format, which are what teams usually blame first.

It's also why she has mostly stopped saying "UGC." Since early 2025 her preferred term has been "creator content," because the person matters more than the label.

## UGC ads vs creator ads vs partnership ads vs whitelisting

UGC and creator content describe the asset, a person filming the product, while whitelisting and partnership ads describe how you run it. With whitelisting, the brand ran ads under the creator's handle but targeted off its own data, so the handle was mostly a mask. Partnership ads run as a joint post, and Dara says they seed delivery from the creator's own audience before Meta expands out. She calls 2022-style whitelisting advice out of date and asks every brand about partnership ads first. We compare the two in [whitelisting vs partnership ads](/blog/whitelisting-vs-partnership-ads).

## The UGC ad formula

Dara's [recipe](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=yNTlVKvuX0o&t=124s) has four ingredients:

1. **A hook that does two jobs.** It stops the viewer, and it tells Meta who to show the ad to, since the first three seconds feed delivery. The hook includes the visual and the text overlay, not only what's said.
2. **A modular script.** Hook, problem agitator, solution, features and benefits, social proof, call to action.
3. **A creator who passes the sniff test.** She says she can usually tell within three seconds of footage.
4. **Plenty of dynamic B-roll**, so the video isn't one static talking head.

The modular script is the part most teams underuse.

**Each module is a swappable part, so one shoot can become many ads.** A hook from one creator can sit on another creator's problem section, and a weak call to action can be replaced without a reshoot.

Say you brief three creators on the same script and ask each for five hook variants. Dara's current guidance is three to six hooks per video. That's 15 finished videos from one brief, which fills one test ad set at her sizing of 10 to 25 ads.

Then someone has to launch them.

At 17+ clicks to build each ad from scratch in Ads Manager, 15 videos is 255 clicks for one ad set. Put them in three ad sets and, if every copy is a rebuild, it's 765.

## UGC ads examples: 9 story structures

The hook gets the attention. The shape of the whole story decides whether anyone buys, and Dara names nine:

1. **Problem-solution.** Her "El Classico" and the safest place for a new brand to start.
2. **Tutorial.** A how-to that reads as organic content. Best for products with obvious utility.
3. **Review.** "This is my honest review of..." Have the creator answer common objections inside it.
4. **Comparison.** Your product against an alternative. She says she sees few and likes them a lot.
5. **Ugly ad.** One take, low production, sometimes Post-it notes.
6. **Day in the life or get ready with me.** Either crushes it or flops, so test it.
7. **Educational.** Listicle-style knowledge, not a step-by-step.
8. **Personal history.** Deep personal storytelling. The most emotional of the nine.
9. **Skit.** Scripted scenes, ideally with more than one person in them.

Unboxings, before/afters and compilations aren't on the list.

She's seen them underperform as standalone ads and work when combined with one of the nine. For testimonial-led versions, see our [testimonial ads examples](/blog/testimonial-ads-examples).

The newest addition is the "yapper" ad: a creator ranting to camera with little B-roll or text. In April 2026 she [posted](https://x.com/DenneyDara/status/2047666276756079091) "Yappers > Traditional UGC by far," while elsewhere calling yappers a trend rather than a permanent change.

Run them now. Don't build your whole program on them.

## How much should you script a UGC creator?

**Brief in inverse proportion to the creator's standing.**

That's Dara's rule, and it settles the script-versus-freedom fight.

A UGC creator paid per video gets a full storyboard: the clip-by-clip script plus the setting and recording rules, loosened only after they've proven themselves. An established influencer gets context and sends back their own concept for approval. An affiliate on commission gets no creative brief at all, because in a [2025 interview](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6i_UTkBrIiU&t=2868s) she says a brief robs them of the freedom they use to convert their own audience.

Won't a full storyboard make a creator sound scripted? Sometimes, and that's the trade-off. The yapper format pushes everyone toward looser scripts.

If the creator can't make the lines sound like their own words, it won't read as a rant. It will read as an ad.

## Where do you find UGC creators?

Start with **Meta's Creator Marketplace**, which Dara notes she isn't sponsored by.

In her [2026 format round-up](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ePcEcT2PH8E&t=794s) she shows it surfacing a creator's organic hook rate and interaction rate, with 50% and 7% as her example figures, plus any past partnership ads. That lets you judge whether someone can carry a yapper ad before you brief them.

Her other sources: scanning TikTok and Reels in your niche, UGC marketplaces such as Tribe and Influy, actor networks when creator content isn't landing, and your own customers, which she calls the highest-variance option of all. Budget roughly $250 to $1,000 per raw piece, by her pay bands.

One warning from the same material.

**Don't hire by follower count.** She retired the micro and macro tiers because Meta's delivery is now personal enough that a creator's community matters more than their reach.

## Why do UGC ads fatigue?

Every UGC ad wears out, and nobody can tell you when.

Dan Pantelo, founder of the catalog-ad company Marpipe, put it plainly in [a 2024 conversation with Dara](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MVg_o8WU6mo): no UGC ad lasts forever, and its decline is unpredictable. His pitch is that catalog ads are the steady line underneath, because they're built from your product feed and matched to shoppers at the product level.

He sells catalog-ad tooling, so weigh it accordingly; the logic still holds.

So what holds the account up when the hero video dies? Keep a catalog campaign running underneath it.

Dara's own answer is breadth. The same creator varies wildly from clip to clip, so you want several creators and longer partnerships, not one star. For the signs that an ad is done, see [what is ad fatigue](/blog/what-is-ad-fatigue).

## Should you use AI UGC avatars?

Not as fake customers.

That's our read of Dara's position. In 2024 she said she avoided deepfake and UGC-clone tools "for candidly ethical reasons," and she hasn't retracted it. Her February 2026 verdict was that [full AI ads are generally a disaster](https://x.com/DenneyDara/status/2018393739060744315), while mixed-media work is producing consistent winners for her team.

Look at the AI she does endorse.

Her animated "AI slop" characters work, in her words, because they aren't trying to pass as real. The AI production tool she rates highest uses real actors and real footage, explicitly not avatars. And in her breakdown of Caraway's creator strategy, she notes that as avatar ads multiply, buyers lean harder on approval from someone they already trust.

That's borrowed trust again. An avatar has none to lend.

Use AI for hook variants and voiceovers, for editing, for research. Keep the face real.

A UGC ad is only as good as the person in it, so the work that pays is finding the right people and testing enough of what they make.

# What Are UGC Ads?

**UGC ads are paid ads built from video made by someone outside the brand, in their own voice and their own setting.** UGC (user-generated content) describes where the video came from, not how it runs. The same clip can run from your brand page, a creator's handle, or a third-party page. And UGC creators aren't influencers: you're paying for a video, not an audience.

That's what UGC ads are. Whether they still work is where experienced Meta buyers start arguing.

Our answer is that generic UGC is tired, but UGC isn't dead. What works now is the right person, the customer's real words, and a native look, made at volume. Below is the evidence for each side, the scripting rule we use, and where AI avatars fit.

## What are UGC ads, exactly?

Most definitions blur two lines that matter.

The first is origin vs vehicle. UGC is the origin: a non-brand person made the video. Partnership ads are a vehicle. So are whitelisting and third-party pages: they're the ways that video reaches the feed. Practitioners disagree a lot more about the vehicle, so it gets its own post: [whitelisting vs partnership ads](/blog/whitelisting-vs-partnership-ads).

The second is the creator's tier, which decides what you're paying for:

| Tier | What you're buying | Their following |
|---|---|---|
| UGC creator | The video and the right to run it | Little or none |
| Creator | The video plus some community trust | Small and engaged |
| Influencer | Their audience and borrowed trust | Large |

**For UGC ads, you buy the asset, not the post.** Negotiate the raw footage and editing rights, plus paid usage with no short time limit. An organic post fades in days; an ad can run for months. Across the market, a creator video costs from about $100 to about $1,000, depending on the creator and the rights.

## Is UGC dead?

No. But one version of it is worn out, and the headline split among experienced buyers is an argument about which version, and what should replace it.

One camp says creators are now core to Meta advertising, and that a brand without a steady supply of real people on camera will struggle to keep reaching new buyers over the next few years. Standard creator content is still among the highest-converting styles for products that need proof, and some accounts put most of their spend behind talking-head creator videos. This camp wins when you can find and keep good creators, the product needs proof, and reaching new people is your constraint.

The other camp says the format is exhausted. One person, phone to face, product in hand, "I'm obsessed" in the first line: everyone has seen it a thousand times. Their fix is to hire trained actors instead, often two at once, shot in a studio. This camp wins when your category is flooded with identical creator testimonials, the format needs two people (an interview, or a skeptic facing a believer), and the budget covers real production.

So which camp is right? Read closely, both agree on the diagnosis.

They disagree on the cure.

**Template UGC is creator content where the person, the script, and the setting could be dropped into any brand's ad without anyone noticing.** That's what's tired. Template UGC is the version even the creator camp has stopped defending.

**Our take: if your creator ads all look the same, change the person, the structure, or the vehicle, not the whole channel.** Deleting creators throws out the part that works.

And if you can't find creators who are believable without a script, actors are a legitimate answer. "UGC is dead" is a contested claim, not a fact.

## What makes UGC ads work now

There are four parts, and template UGC fails all four.

### 1. The right person

Practitioners agree the person on camera is the biggest variable in a UGC ad. Same product, same offer, different person, different result.

So use several creators, not one. Start with a few and keep adding new faces every month.

Screen in the first few seconds of a sample. Look for a voice that moves and confidence that doesn't oversell. Monotone script-reading and diving into features with no story are the red flags. Recruit by who they are ("parents of toddlers," "runners over 40"), not by who replied first.

Does the creator need to look like the buyer? It helps. But a lookalike with flat delivery still loses, so delivery comes first.

### 2. Real words

The best lines come from customers, not from the brief writer. Mine reviews and ad comments for the odd, specific detail a real buyer used, then hand it to the creator as something to say in their own words rather than a line to read off a card. Our [testimonial ads examples](/blog/testimonial-ads-examples) show how to find those lines and why a converted skeptic beats a fan.

### 3. A native look

UGC works because it doesn't look like an ad. Native doesn't mean cheap, though. For low-ticket products, phone-shot is right, and the savings buy more creators. For high-ticket or high-trust products, pay for casting, sound, and setting while keeping the conversational look. Glossy and branded is the one option nobody defends.

### 4. Volume

**One shoot should become many ads before anyone reshoots.** Ask for several hooks in the same session. Cut each raw clip into several variants, lay new footage over the middle, swap the setting, then reshoot a winning script with a new face. That matters because UGC wears out in three separate ways (the ad itself, the footage, the face), and you can refresh each one on its own long before you need to book another shoot.

## UGC ads examples that still work

These formats keep showing up in accounts that run creator content at scale:

- **The talking-head rant.** One person, loosely briefed, venting about a problem before the product appears late. Industry slang calls it a "yapper" video.
- **The real-room demo.** The product doing its job in an ordinary kitchen or bathroom. Track the room as a variable in your ad names, because a creator in a bright, lived-in kitchen and the same creator in a bare rental can produce two very different ads.
- **The converted skeptic.** The creator opens on the doubt they had and walks through what changed their mind.
- **The two-person format.** An interview or a skeptic-and-believer exchange. It needs people who can deliver a script, usually actors or proven creators.

## Should you script UGC ads?

Experienced buyers split here too.

The loose camp gives creators a hook and three talking points and nothing more. Over-scripted creators sound like robots, and rigid brand books make every video converge on the same ad. The tight camp still writes clip-by-clip storyboards and treats a winning script as the reusable asset: when one tops spend, they hand it to a new creator.

Both are right, for different people.

We resolve it with one rule.

**Brief depth goes down as creator tier goes up.** A new UGC creator gets a full storyboard. An established creator gets the hook and talking points, plus the proof you need on screen. Influencers get the goal and the no-go list. Affiliates often get nothing at all.

The verdict underneath: script the structure and the proof, then free the delivery. A tight script needs someone who can act it. A loose brief needs someone who's good on camera without one.

## Can AI avatars replace UGC creators?

Not yet, and practitioners split three ways on how far to use them.

The first camp runs AI actors in live ads, with rules: match the avatar to the customer and never let it claim personal use. The second tests scripts and angles with AI, because a synthetic presenter costs almost nothing per take, then shoots the winners with real people once the numbers say which script deserves a real creator's time. The third avoids synthetic people wherever the human is the point, because a creator submitting AI footage defeats the purpose of hiring one.

Across the practitioners we read, realistic AI isn't yet beating real people shot well.

**Our take: test with synthetic people if you like, then reshoot the winners with real people.** Never use a synthetic expert, and never a synthetic "user" saying "I tried this and it worked."

Nobody, human or AI, claims personal experience they don't have. Check current labeling and disclosure rules before any synthetic person goes live. Our guide to [AI for Facebook ads](/blog/ai-for-facebook-ads) covers the rest of the workflow.

## What to stop doing

- **Picking creators by follower count.** Delivery and fit predict results better than audience size.
- **One-off, churn-and-burn creator deals.** Results come from several creators over longer partnerships, judged over weeks.
- **Calling whitelisting the old name for partnership ads.** Plenty of buyers run both, as different mechanics.

## Every shoot becomes many ads to launch

Every move above is a multiplier. Say you book three creators and cut each shoot into six variants. That's 18 ads. Test each in four ad sets and you have 72.

In Ads Manager, building one ad from scratch in one ad set takes 17+ clicks. If you rebuild each of the 72, that's 1,224 clicks for one round of creator content. Almost none of it is thinking.

Then there's the read. Put who's on camera, which hook they used, and where they shot it into each file name (`maya_price-hook_kitchen_v2`), and a month later you can see which person and which cut carried spend.
